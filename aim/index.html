Reaction Test

Aim test

Twenty targets appear one at a time in the box. Tap each one as fast as you can.

Loading...

How the test works

  1. Tap the box to start. A round target appears somewhere inside it.
  2. Tap it. It vanishes and the next one appears in a new place.
  3. After twenty targets, the test shows your average time per target in milliseconds and the number of taps that missed.

The clock starts with your first tap and runs until the twentieth target is hit, so time lost on misses counts against you. The targets are the same size throughout.

What it measures

Hitting a target takes three steps: seeing where it is, moving the finger or pointer to it, and landing accurately enough. The first step is reaction time; the second and third are movement. Your time per target is therefore always longer than your plain reaction time, and the difference grows when the next target is far from the last one.

There is a regular pattern here, known to anyone who designs screens and buttons: a target takes longer to hit the farther away and the smaller it is. That is why important buttons are made large and placed where the finger already rests.

Touch and mouse are different tests

On a phone you lift a finger and put it down; on a computer you slide a mouse and click. The two use different muscles and have different difficulties, and people who are quick at one are not always quick at the other. A touchpad is usually slower than either. Keep your comparisons to one kind of device. On a phone, the size of the box depends on the screen, so a small phone gives shorter distances than a tablet.

How to improve

  • Look first, then move. Let your eyes jump to the new target before your hand follows. Moving while still searching wastes more time than it saves.
  • Do not hurry the landing. A miss costs far more than a slightly slower hit, because you must aim again.
  • Hold the device steady. On a phone, rest it on a table or grip it firmly and use one finger of the other hand.
  • With a mouse, rest your forearm on the desk and move from the wrist for short distances and the elbow for long ones. A mouse set too fast is hard to stop on target; many people aim better after turning the pointer speed down a little.
  • Warm up. The second and third attempts are usually better than the first.

Accuracy matters too

Look at the misses as well as the time. A score of 600 with no misses is a better performance than 550 with six. If you are missing more than two or three in twenty, slow down by a little; your time will often improve.

Who uses aim practice

Players of fast computer games practise aiming with a mouse. But the skill is an everyday one: touch typing on glass, using a tablet for drawing, operating a ticket machine. Children practise the same hand-eye skill with every ball they catch.

A game for fun and practice. It is not a test of eyesight or coordination in any medical sense.
