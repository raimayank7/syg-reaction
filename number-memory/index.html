Number memory test

A number appears for a few seconds, then hides. Type it. Each round adds a digit.

Loading...

How the test works

  1. Tap to start. A three-digit number appears.
  2. It stays for a few seconds, a little longer for longer numbers, and then disappears.
  3. Type the number and tap Check.
  4. If you are right, the next number has one more digit. The test ends at your first mistake, and your score is the length of the longest number you got right.

What it measures

This is a version of the digit span test, one of the oldest measures in psychology. In the classic form an examiner reads digits aloud at a steady pace and the listener repeats them. Here you read them instead, and you can look at the whole number at once, which most people find slightly easier.

Why about seven?

Most adults can repeat a string of about seven digits, give or take two. The observation is famous from a paper published in the 1950s by the American psychologist George Miller, who pointed out how often "seven, plus or minus two" turned up as a limit of immediate memory. Later work suggests that the true limit is smaller, about four "chunks", and that we reach seven digits by silently grouping and rehearsing them. Either way, the practical lesson is the same: short-term memory is small, and what you do with the digits matters more than raw capacity.

Chunking: the method that works

A chunk is a group that you treat as one thing. Nobody remembers a ten-digit phone number as ten separate digits; we say it in groups. Do the same here:

  • Group in threes or fours. 582914 becomes "582, 914".
  • Say it to yourself in a rhythm, as you would a phone number. Hearing your inner voice is a second memory on top of seeing.
  • Find meaning. 1947 is a year. 786, 108 and 420 mean something to many Indians. 2468 is a pattern. Any meaning turns four digits into one chunk.
  • Use what you know: cricket scores, PIN codes of towns, bus numbers, birthdays.

With practice at grouping, people commonly add several digits to their score. Trained memory competitors go much further by turning groups of digits into pictures and placing the pictures along an imagined route, but that is a skill learned over months.

Fair play

No writing the number down, no photographing the screen, no saying it aloud to a friend who types it. Whispering it to yourself is allowed; it is how memory works.

Things that lower your score

  • Interruption. Any speech, music with words or a notification between seeing and typing can wipe the digits.
  • Similar digits. Strings with repeats such as 7737 are easy to scramble.
  • Typing slips. Check what you typed before pressing Check.
  • Tiredness.

Uses in real life

The same skill carries a one-time password from a message to a form, a phone number from a hoarding to your dialler, and a railway PNR from the ticket to the enquiry screen. Chunking makes all of them easier: read an OTP as two groups of three and you will rarely need a second look.

A game for fun. It is not a clinical memory test and says nothing about your health.
