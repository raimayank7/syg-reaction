Memory span and chunking

Read this number once and look away: 4 9 2 7 1 8 3 6 5 0 2. Most people lose it somewhere after the seventh digit. Yet you can easily remember "1947 1971 2011", which is longer. The difference between the two is the most useful thing to know about your own memory, and it is what our three memory tests are quietly teaching.

A small workbench

Psychologists distinguish between long-term memory, the vast store of what you know, and short-term or working memory, the small workbench where you hold things for a few seconds while using them: a phone number between reading and dialling, the start of a sentence while you hear its end, the carried figure in a sum. The workbench is small, and anything left on it without attention fades or is pushed off by the next arrival.

How small?

The number of items a person can repeat back in order straight after hearing them is called the memory span. For digits, most adults manage around seven, give or take two. This observation was made famous in the 1950s by the psychologist George Miller. Later research argues that the real capacity is about four units, and that we stretch it to seven digits by grouping and by silently repeating them. Span grows through childhood and differs from person to person, and for words it is shorter than for digits.

Chunks: the unit that matters

The workbench counts chunks, not digits or letters. A chunk is anything you can treat as a single familiar unit. The letters C, B, S, E, I, S, R, O are eight things to a stranger and two to an Indian student: CBSE and ISRO. The year 1947 is one chunk, not four. The more you know, the bigger your chunks, and the more you can hold. This is why experts seem to have superhuman memories in their own field. A chess master remembers a real game position at a glance, because it breaks into familiar groups of pieces; shown pieces scattered at random, the master does little better than a beginner.

Ways to chunk

  • Group by rhythm. Say digits in threes or fours with a pause, as with a phone number.
  • Find meaning. Years, cricket scores, PIN codes, bus routes, prices.
  • Find patterns. 2468, 1357, 9090, runs and repeats.
  • Turn positions into shapes. In sequence memory and tile memory, lines, corners and letters of the alphabet.
  • Recode. Number the nine squares like a keypad and a sequence of places becomes a phone number.

Rehearsal and the inner voice

We keep words and digits alive by repeating them under our breath. Because this repetition takes time, you can hold about as much as you can say to yourself in roughly two seconds, which is why short, quick-to-say items are held better than long ones. It also explains why speech is so destructive: if someone talks to you, or you have to speak, while holding a number, the inner voice is interrupted and the number goes. Pictures and positions are held by a different, visual part of the workbench, so humming a tune disturbs your memory for a number far more than your memory for a pattern of tiles.

First and last are remembered best

In any list, people recall the first few items and the last few better than those in the middle. The first few got the most rehearsal; the last few are still fresh. The middle has neither advantage. When watching a long sequence, give the middle your attention.

Making things last

Holding something for ten seconds is one skill; keeping it for a week is another. To move something into long-term memory, connect it to what you already know, and recall it again after a gap: after a minute, after an hour, the next day. Testing yourself is more effective than re-reading. These habits matter far more for study than a large span does.

Using this in daily life

  • One-time passwords: read six digits as two groups of three.
  • Phone numbers: always in groups, and say them aloud once.
  • Shopping without a list: group items by where they are in the shop, or make a silly picture that links them.
  • Directions: turn "left, second right, left" into a shape or a short phrase.
  • When it matters, write it down. The workbench is for seconds, not for safekeeping.

What the games do and do not show

Practising our tests will make you better at our tests, mainly because you learn to chunk. That is worth knowing and fun to watch. It has not been shown that practising memory games makes people cleverer in general or protects memory in later life, and we make no such claim. A low score on a bad day means nothing at all.
