Reaction Test

Tile memory test

Some tiles flash, then hide. Tap the tiles that were lit. Three mistakes end the test.

Loading...

How the test works

  1. Tap to start. Three tiles of a three by three grid light up together for about a second.
  2. They go dark. Tap the tiles that were lit, in any order.
  3. Find them all and the next round shows one more tile. As the number grows, so does the grid: four by four, then five by five, then six by six.
  4. Each wrong tile costs a life. You have three lives for the whole test. Your score is the largest pattern you completed.

What it measures

This is a test of visual pattern memory: holding a picture in mind for a few seconds after it has gone. Unlike sequence memory, order does not matter and everything is shown at once. Psychologists treat the memory for "what was where" as partly separate from the memory for words and digits, which is why someone with a modest score on number memory may do very well here, and the other way round.

How to remember a pattern

  • See shapes, not tiles. Six lit tiles are hard to hold; "a diagonal line and a small square" is easy. Look for lines, corners, L-shapes, steps and pairs.
  • Use the gaps. Sometimes the unlit tiles make the simpler shape: "everything except the top row".
  • Take in the whole grid. Fix your eyes on the centre and do not chase individual tiles. The flash is too short to look at each one.
  • Name it. Give the shape a word: "a chair", "the letter T", "a staircase". A name is a hook.
  • Tap the sure ones first. Begin with the group you remember best, and leave the doubtful tile for last, when the others on the board may jog your memory.
  • Do not guess wildly. With three lives for the whole game, a careless tap in an early round costs you later, when you need the margin.

Why it gets hard so quickly

With three tiles on nine squares there are 84 possible patterns. With eight tiles on a five by five grid there are over a million. Yet the flash lasts barely longer. What saves you is that most random patterns contain some accidental shape you can hold on to. Your score is, in effect, a measure of how good you are at finding structure in a jumble in one second.

A skill people use

  • A chess player glancing at a board remembers it by patterns of pieces, not square by square.
  • Recognising a constellation, or where the cards lie in a game of pairs.
  • Remembering where you left things on a crowded table.
  • Copying a rangoli or kolam dot pattern after one look.

Play it with others

Take turns on one phone and compare scores, or play the oldest version of all with a tray: put a dozen small things on a tray, show it for half a minute, cover it, and see who can list the most. The tray game is described on our neighbour site Group Games.

A game for fun. It is not an eyesight or memory assessment.
