Reaction Test

Why your score differs from device to device

Take a reaction test on a phone, then on a laptop, then on a friend's tablet, and you will get three different averages. You did not change; the devices did. Every reaction test on a screen measures you plus the machine. Knowing where the machine's share comes from helps you read your scores sensibly, and compare them only when the comparison is fair.

What the page actually measures

The test page notes the time at which it asked the screen to turn green, and the time at which the browser told it about your tap. Your true reaction time lies inside that interval, with delay on both sides.

Delay before you can see the signal

  • Waiting for the next frame. A screen redraws itself a fixed number of times a second. Most screens refresh 60 times a second, so a new picture can appear only every 16.7 milliseconds; a 120-hertz screen halves that to 8.3. The green has to wait for the next refresh, and on average waits about half a frame.
  • The display's own lag. After receiving a new frame, the panel takes some time to change its pixels. Televisions with picture processing switched on can be especially slow, which is why they have a "game mode".
  • The browser and system. A busy device, with many tabs open or power saving switched on, may be a frame or two late.

Delay after you move

  • Touch sensing. A touch screen checks for fingers a fixed number of times a second, and then the system has to work out that it was a tap. This adds anything from a few milliseconds to a few tens of milliseconds, depending on the phone.
  • Mouse and keyboard. A wired mouse reports quickly. A wireless or Bluetooth mouse may add several milliseconds, and some add more. Laptop touchpads are usually slowest, especially if "tap to click" has to wait to see whether you are making a double tap.
  • The button itself. A mouse button must travel before it registers. A finger on glass registers on contact.

Sound has its own delay

For the sound test, the device must play the beep. Audio output is buffered, and the delay ranges from a few milliseconds on a good system to several tens of milliseconds on others. Bluetooth earphones and speakers commonly add a tenth of a second or more, because the sound is compressed, sent by radio and unpacked again. That is easily enough to make your sound score look slower than your light score, although ears are in fact quicker than eyes.

How much in total?

It varies too much for one honest figure. As a rough picture, the delays of a typical phone or computer add some tens of milliseconds to a visual reaction score, and a slow television or a wireless set-up can add much more. This is why we do not print a league table of "excellent" and "poor" scores, and why claims that someone's phone score "beats a fighter pilot" mean nothing.

How to compare fairly

  • Same device, same browser, same hand. Then the delay is the same every time and cancels out when you compare.
  • Close other apps and tabs. Plug in a laptop, or switch off battery saver.
  • Use averages. One try is noise. Our tests average five; average several sessions if you are doing an experiment.
  • Throw away practice rounds.
  • When comparing people, pass one device around.
  • For sound, use the built-in speaker or wired earphones.

A way to estimate your device's share

Do the ruler drop test ten times with a friend and work out your average reaction time from the table there. The ruler has no electronics and no delay. Then do our screen test several times. The difference between the two averages is a fair estimate of the delay your device adds, give or take the slight difference between catching and tapping. Classes that try this usually find the phone figure higher than the ruler figure, as expected.

Does this make screen tests pointless?

No. A bathroom scale that reads one kilogram too heavy still tells you whether you are gaining or losing. A reaction test with a constant delay still shows whether you are faster after practice, slower when tired, quicker to sound than to light. Those comparisons are where the interest lies.
