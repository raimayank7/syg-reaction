Reaction Test

How to test fairly: a method for small experiments

A single reaction score is a snapshot taken on a moving bus. It jumps about from try to try for reasons that have nothing to do with what you want to know. If you want a real answer to a question such as "am I faster in the morning?" or "is my left hand slower?", you need a method. The method is simple, it is the same one scientists use, and it makes a good school or holiday project.

1. Ask one clear question

Good questions compare two conditions and change only one thing:

  • Right hand or left hand?
  • Light or sound?
  • Morning or night?
  • Before or after ten minutes of exercise?
  • Sitting quietly or listening to a story at the same time?
  • Phone or laptop? (This one measures the devices, not you.)

2. Keep everything else the same

Same device, same browser, same chair, same finger, same brightness. If two things change at once, you cannot tell which one made the difference. This is the most important rule, and the one most often broken.

3. Warm up, and do not count it

The first tries of any session are slower while you settle. Do one whole practice round of five and ignore it.

4. Take enough measurements

Each of our tests averages five tries. For an experiment, do at least three rounds in each condition, fifteen tries in all. More is better. A difference that shows up once may be chance; a difference that shows up in most of ten rounds is probably real.

5. Alternate the order

If you always test the right hand first and the left hand second, the left hand also gets the benefit of practice, or the cost of tiredness. So alternate: right, left, left, right. Or do one order today and the other tomorrow.

7. Use the middle value

Reaction times have a peculiar shape: most are bunched together, with now and then one very slow try when your attention wandered. One such try can drag an average a long way. The median, the middle value when the scores are put in order, is not disturbed by it. With five rounds, sort the five scores and take the third. Compare medians.

8. Be honest about small differences

Do the same condition twice and you will get two different medians. That difference is the size of your "noise". If conditions A and B differ by no more than the noise, the honest conclusion is "no difference that I could detect". That is a real result, and scientists report it often.

9. Deal fairly with odd tries

Decide before you start what you will do with a try that is spoilt, for instance when someone spoke to you. The fair rule: repeat a spoilt try immediately and note it; never delete a score just because you do not like it.

Ideas for projects

  • A class survey. Everyone does three rounds on the same device. Plot the medians as a bar chart. Is there a pattern by age within the family? (Compare groups, never tease individuals.)
  • Does a warning help? Have a friend say "ready" one second before you start each try, and compare with no warning.
  • Does talking slow you down? Compare silent rounds with rounds in which you count backwards aloud in threes. Most people are clearly slower. This is the simplest demonstration of why a driver should not use a phone.
  • Screen against ruler. Compare with the ruler drop test to estimate your device's delay, as explained in why scores differ.

Where your scores are kept

This site keeps your recent scores for each test in your browser and lists them on the my scores page. They never leave your device. For an experiment, a paper table is still better, because you can label each round with its condition.

These are games and classroom experiments. Do not use them to judge anyone's health, ability or fitness to drive.

The tests

  • Reaction time
    Tap when it turns green
  • Sound reaction
    Tap when you hear the beep
  • Go / no-go
    Tap for green, not for orange
  • Aim
    Hit 20 targets
  • Tap speed
    Taps in 10 seconds
  • Sequence memory
    Repeat a growing pattern
  • Number memory
    Remember longer numbers
  • Tile memory
    Remember which tiles lit up
