Reaction Test

How far you travel before you react

While you are reacting, the world does not wait. A cyclist, a car or a train keeps moving at full speed during the time it takes a person to notice a danger and begin to act. How far it travels in that time is plain arithmetic, speed multiplied by time, and the answers are larger than most people expect. This page does the arithmetic. It is not a road-safety manual and gives no official stopping distances; for those, read the driving manual and road rules of your own country or state.

The sum

To turn kilometres per hour into metres per second, divide by 3.6. At 60 km/h a vehicle covers 16.7 metres every second; at 100 km/h, 27.8 metres. Multiply by the time and you have the distance covered before anything has been done.

The first rows are walking pace, a child's bicycle and a slow street; the last are highway speeds. The distance grows in step with speed: double the speed, double the distance covered while reacting.

Why real reactions on the road take so long

On our reaction time test you know a signal is coming, you know what it will be, you are staring at the spot and your finger is ready. A person on a road has none of those advantages. They must first notice that something is wrong, which may need the eyes to move; recognise what it is; decide what to do; and then move a foot from one pedal to another before any braking begins. Each stage takes time, and the whole takes far longer than a laboratory reaction, commonly a second or more. This is why people who design roads and teach driving allow much more time than a reaction test would suggest.

And this is only the distance covered before braking starts. The distance needed to stop once the brakes are on is extra, and it grows with the square of the speed.

What lengthens the time

  • Looking away. A glance at a phone of two seconds at 60 km/h is 33 metres travelled without looking, as the table shows.
  • Divided attention. A conversation or a message occupies the same decision-making that the road needs. Try our go / no-go test while counting backwards aloud and see what happens to your time and your mistakes.
  • Tiredness, alcohol and many medicines.
  • Surprise. The unexpected is answered more slowly than the expected.
  • Poor light, rain and glare, which delay noticing.

What shortens it

  • Expecting trouble. A person who has already noticed the child near the kerb, and is half prepared, responds much sooner.
  • Looking far ahead, so that there is more warning.
  • Lower speed. It does not shorten the reaction, but it shortens the distance covered during it, and that is what matters.
  • Space. Leaving a gap to the vehicle in front gives time that no reflex can.

Not only vehicles

  • A cricket ball at 140 km/h covers nearly 8 metres in a fifth of a second; see reaction time in sport.
  • A cyclist at 15 km/h covers about 4 metres in a second, the width of a small room, before even touching the brakes.
  • A person running at 15 km/h on a wet pool-side or a crowded platform has the same 4 metres.

A demonstration for a class

  1. Mark a line in the playground. Choose a speed from the table, such as a bicycle at 15 km/h.
  2. Pace out the distance covered in one second and in two seconds, and put markers there.
  3. Ask pupils to guess the distances first. Most guess far too short.
  4. Repeat on paper for 50 km/h, the length of a few classrooms, to feel what a moment's inattention means on a street.

What our tests cannot tell you

A fast score on a tapping test does not make anyone a safe driver or rider, and a slow score does not make anyone unsafe. Safe driving depends on attention, anticipation, speed and space far more than on reflexes. These pages are for understanding, not for judging fitness to drive.

Always follow your local road rules and the official driving manual. This page shows arithmetic only and gives no advice on driving.
