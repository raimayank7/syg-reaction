Reaction Test

The ruler drop test

You can measure reaction time with nothing but a 30-centimetre ruler and a friend. No screen, no battery, and none of the delays that phones add. The method has been used in school science lessons for generations, and it works because a falling object keeps perfect time: the farther the ruler falls before you catch it, the longer you took to react.

What you need

  • A ruler of 30 cm (a longer one, or a metre stick, is even better for children)
  • A partner
  • Paper and pen

How to do it

  1. Sit with your forearm resting on a table, hand over the edge, thumb and forefinger open about two centimetres apart.
  2. Your partner holds the ruler upright by its top end, so that the zero mark is level with the top of your thumb, between your finger and thumb but not touching them.
  3. Without warning, your partner lets go. They must not say anything or count down, and should vary the wait between two and ten seconds.
  4. Catch the ruler between finger and thumb as fast as you can.
  5. Read the centimetre mark at the top of your thumb. That is how far the ruler fell.
  6. Repeat ten times and write down every catch. If the ruler falls right through, write "missed" and try again.
  7. Swap places.

Turning centimetres into time

A dropped object falls a distance of half of g times the time squared, where g is the acceleration of gravity, 9.81 metres per second per second. Turned round, the time is the square root of (2 times the distance, divided by g). The table does the sum for you.

So a catch at 15 cm means about 175 milliseconds, and a catch at 20 cm about 202. Notice that the time does not rise in step with the distance: the ruler speeds up as it falls, so the second ten centimetres pass much more quickly than the first. A full 30 cm ruler measures reactions up to about a quarter of a second; if you keep missing, use a longer stick.

Getting a fair result

  • Take the middle value. Put your ten catches in order and take the average of the fifth and sixth. One lucky or dozy catch will not disturb it.
  • Ignore the first two or three as practice.
  • No anticipating. If you close your fingers before the ruler is released, it does not count. The dropper should watch for this.
  • Same starting gap between finger and thumb each time; a wider gap takes longer to close.
  • Start at zero. If the ruler's scale does not begin at its end, line up the zero mark, not the end.
  • Rest the arm. A hand that drops with the ruler gives a falsely long reading.

What to expect

Catches by adults and older children usually fall somewhere in the middle of the ruler, which corresponds to roughly 150 to 220 milliseconds in the table. Younger children are slower. Remember that this is a catch, a pinching movement you can prepare in advance, and that you are watching the dropper's hand, which may give tiny clues. So the ruler figure tends to be a little quicker than a light-and-tap test.

Experiments to try

  • Sight against touch. Close your eyes; the dropper lets the ruler rest lightly against your finger, so that you feel it start to move. Then again with eyes open and no touching. Then with eyes closed and the dropper saying "now" as they release, to test hearing. Which sense is fastest?
  • Which hand? Compare your writing hand with the other.
  • Distraction. Catch while counting backwards from 100 in sevens.
  • Practice. Do thirty catches and compare the first ten with the last ten.
  • Screen against ruler. Compare with our reaction time test. The gap between the two is partly your device's delay; see why scores differ.

Whatever you test, change one thing at a time and write everything down: how to test fairly.

A trick for parties

Hold a currency note upright by its top edge with its middle between a friend's open finger and thumb, and tell them they may keep it if they catch it. A note is about the right length to be gone before a typical reaction is complete; the table shows that a fall of half its length takes little more than a tenth of a second. Almost nobody catches it unless they guess. Do not play it for money, and take the note back afterwards.

For teachers

The activity covers measurement, repeated trials, averages, a formula with a square root and the idea of a fair test, in one lesson, with equipment every pupil owns. Younger classes can skip the formula and simply compare centimetres: a shorter catch is a faster reaction. Older classes can derive the formula, plot time against distance and discuss why the scale on a "reaction ruler" would not be evenly spaced.
