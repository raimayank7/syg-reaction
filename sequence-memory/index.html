Reaction Test

Sequence memory test

Watch the squares light up, then tap them in the same order. Each round adds one more step.

Loading...

How the test works

  1. Tap to start. One of the nine squares lights up.
  2. When the message says "your turn", tap that square.
  3. The pattern is then shown again with one more step added at the end. Repeat the whole pattern from the beginning.
  4. The test ends at your first wrong tap. Your score is the longest pattern you repeated correctly.

The pattern keeps its earlier steps; only one new step is added each round. A square may be used more than once, even twice in a row.

What it measures

This is a test of short-term memory for positions in order, sometimes called a spatial span task. A test of the same kind, in which an examiner taps blocks in a sequence for a person to copy, has been used by psychologists for many decades. Because the pattern here builds up one step at a time and you rehearse it every round, most people get further than they would with a brand-new pattern each time.

How people remember sequences

Short-term memory holds only a handful of separate things at once. People who score well are not holding twelve separate positions; they are grouping them into a few larger pieces. Here are ways to do that:

  • Shapes. See the steps as a path drawn on the grid: "an L, then a diagonal, then back to the middle".
  • Numbers. Number the squares like a phone keypad, 1 to 9, and remember the pattern as a phone number in groups of three or four digits: "159, 357, 2".
  • Rhythm. Tap it back in a beat, in groups: da-da-da, da-da-da. A rhythm glues steps together.
  • Words or stories. Name the squares (top-left "tree", centre "home") and make a sentence.
  • Hand memory. Trace the path in the air with a finger while watching. Many people find their hand remembers what their head has lost.

Try one method for a few games before judging it. The keypad method is the favourite of most high scorers.

Where it goes wrong

  • In the middle. The first and the last few steps are remembered best; the middle of a long sequence blurs. Give the middle extra attention while watching.
  • Repeats. The same square twice in a row is easy to miss. Watch for the second flash.
  • Hurrying. There is no time limit for your turn. Tap steadily.
  • Looking away. One glance at a message on your phone and the pattern is gone. That, too, is a finding about short-term memory: it is easily overwritten.

Play it as a group

Pass the phone round: each player repeats the pattern and the next round's addition, and whoever slips is out. Or play the spoken version without any device: the first player says a colour, the next repeats it and adds one, and so on round the circle. It is a good game for a car journey.

A game for fun. It is not a memory assessment, and a low or high score says nothing about your memory in daily life or your health.
