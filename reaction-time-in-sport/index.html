Reaction Test

Reaction time in cricket and other sports

A fast bowler's delivery reaches the batter in under half a second. A simple reaction to a light takes about a fifth of a second even in a laboratory, and deciding which shot to play takes longer still. Put those two facts together and it seems that batting against real pace ought to be impossible. It is not, and the reason tells you what skill in fast sports really is.

How long does the batter have?

A cricket pitch is 22 yards, or 20.12 metres, from one set of stumps to the other. The bowler lets go of the ball near one popping crease and the batter stands near the other; the two creases are 58 feet apart, which is 17.68 metres. If we ignore the slowing of the ball through the air and off the pitch, the time for the ball to cover that distance is simply distance divided by speed:

The real ball loses some speed on the way, especially when it bounces, so the true times are a little longer than these. But the picture is right: at 140 km/h the batter has less than half a second for everything.

Where the half second goes

Seeing the ball and beginning any movement takes roughly a fifth of a second. Swinging a bat takes time as well. So the choice of shot must be made early in the ball's flight, and for the last part of the flight the batter is committed: there is no longer time to see something new and change the stroke. A late swing or an odd bounce beats the bat for exactly this reason. It happens inside the time when nothing can be done.

So how do they do it? They do not wait for the ball

Studies of skilled players in cricket, tennis, baseball and other fast sports keep finding the same thing: experts are not much quicker than other people on a plain reaction test. What they have is anticipation. A good batter reads the bowler's run-up, the position of the wrist and seam, and the first few metres of flight, and from thousands of hours of experience predicts the line and length before most of the flight has happened. The stroke is prepared in advance and adjusted at the last moment. The same is true of a goalkeeper reading a penalty-taker's hips, and of a tennis player reading the server's toss.

This is also why a bowler who disguises the delivery, or who has an unfamiliar action, is so effective. The batter's prediction is wrong or late, and raw reaction is not fast enough to rescue it.

Wherever the time is around a quarter of a second or less, the defender cannot succeed by reacting to the ball alone. A goalkeeper facing a hard penalty must choose a side from the kicker's movements or simply guess. A table tennis player at the table must read the opponent's bat angle and body.

The sprint start

Sprinters react to the starter's gun, a sound, because we respond a little faster to sound than to sight. Athletics officials use sensors in the starting blocks, and a start that comes sooner after the gun than a human could have reacted is ruled a false start: such a start must have been a guess. It is a rule built directly on the science of reaction time.

What this means for training

  • Watch the right thing. Coaches teach where to look: the bowler's hand at release, the kicker's standing foot, the server's toss.
  • Experience is the real speed. Facing many deliveries, or watching them closely, builds the store of patterns that anticipation depends on.
  • Preparation shortens the response. A balanced, ready stance removes movement time.
  • Plain reaction drills help only a little. Being quicker on a light-and-tap test, such as ours, does not by itself make anyone a better batter.

Try it yourself

Measure your simple reaction with the reaction time test and your decision time with the go / no-go test. Then compare your numbers with the table above, remembering that a phone adds its own delay. You will have a new respect for anyone who middles a ball at 140 km/h.
