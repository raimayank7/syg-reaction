Reaction Test

What is reaction time?

Reaction time is the gap between something happening and your starting to respond to it: between the green light and your finger, the starter's gun and the sprinter's push, the ball leaving the bowler's hand and the batter's first movement. It feels instant. It is not. Even at your quickest, a noticeable fraction of a second passes, and understanding where that time goes explains a great deal about sport, safety and the scores on this site.

Where the time goes

  1. Sensing. The eye or ear turns light or sound into nerve signals.
  2. Travelling in. The signals travel along nerves to the brain.
  3. Recognising and deciding. The brain identifies the signal and selects a response. In the simplest test there is hardly any decision; in real life this is often the longest stage.
  4. Travelling out. The command travels down the spinal cord and along nerves to the muscles.
  5. Moving. The muscles tighten and the finger starts to move.

Nerve signals are fast but not instant, and each relay between nerve cells adds a little delay. Added together, the stages come to roughly a fifth to a quarter of a second for a simple response to a light in a healthy young adult measured with laboratory equipment. That is about the length of a blink.

Three kinds of reaction time

  • Simple reaction time. One signal, one response. Our reaction time test and sound test are of this kind.
  • Go / no-go. Respond to one signal, hold back for another. See the go / no-go test.
  • Choice reaction time. Several signals, each with its own response, as when a keeper must dive left or right. The more alternatives there are, the longer the response takes; reaction time rises steadily each time the number of choices doubles. This regularity was worked out in the 1950s and is one of the most dependable results in psychology.

What makes it shorter or longer

  • The sense used. Responses to sound and touch are a little quicker than responses to light.
  • How strong the signal is. A bright flash or loud bang is answered sooner than a faint one.
  • Being ready. A warning just before the signal ("ready, steady...") shortens the response. Not knowing when or whether it will come lengthens it.
  • Practice. Practice at a particular task makes that task faster, especially when a decision is involved. A wicketkeeper is not blessed with faster nerves; a keeper has learnt what to look for.
  • Age. Reaction time shortens through childhood, is quickest in young adulthood and lengthens slowly with age.
  • Tiredness and distraction. Both lengthen it, and distraction lengthens it most when a decision is needed.

Reaction is not the same as speed of movement

Reaction time ends when the movement begins. How long the movement then takes is a separate matter, called movement time. A boxer's punch, or a hand catching a ball, involves both. Our aim test mixes the two; the tap speed test is almost all movement.

Anticipation beats reaction

If the fastest possible reaction takes about a fifth of a second, how does anyone return a fast serve or play fast bowling? The answer is that experts do not wait for the ball. They read cues that come earlier, such as the bowler's wrist, the server's toss or the angle of the hips, and start preparing the right response before the ball is released. Studies of skilled players in many sports find that their simple reaction times are not very different from other people's. What differs is how early they know what is coming. See reaction time in cricket and other sports.

What a home test can and cannot tell you

A web page can measure the time between showing a signal and receiving your tap. That includes your reaction time and also the delays of the screen and the touch sensor, which differ from device to device; see why scores differ by device. So a home test is good for comparing yourself with yourself, for experiments and for fun. It cannot diagnose anything, cannot certify anyone as a safe driver or a good athlete, and should not be used to judge another person.

Try it without a screen

The oldest reaction test needs only a ruler and a friend: the ruler drop test. It has no device delay at all, which makes it a nice check on the numbers a phone gives you.
